# UX psychology principles

Users don't make logical decisions. Design for how they actually think. Source: UX Peak, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2TlIg3VokY8

## 1. Smart Defaults — never show a blank form

Pre-fill every field with the most common choice. 70–90% of users never change defaults; a default reads as a recommendation. User's job becomes "scan and adjust", not "fill from scratch". More choices = harder (jam study: 24 flavors → 3% bought; 6 flavors → 30%). Buttons should show outcome ("12 results waiting"), not verbs ("Search").

## 2. Goal Gradient — never start at 0%

The closer people feel to a goal, the faster they move (car-wash stamp study: pre-stamped card ≈ 2× completion). Reframe an already-done step (account creation) as progress: start onboarding at 20% with step 1 checked. LinkedIn profile-strength meter never shows zero.

## 3. Reciprocity — give value BEFORE the signup wall

Never blur/lock results behind "create an account". Deliver a genuinely useful partial result first (score, top issues), then ask: "Want the full breakdown? Save your report." Receiving first creates the pull to return the favor (Cialdini's strongest persuasion driver). Pattern: Spotify 30-day trial, Notion full product before pay.

## 4. IKEA Effect — let users build before signup

People value what they made themselves; ownership feeling alone suffices (endowment effect). Move personalization BEFORE account creation: pick name, color, style, first lesson (Duolingo). Button says "Continue", not "Sign up" — leaving now feels like abandoning something they built.

## 5. Loss Aversion — frame as preventing loss, not adding gain

Losing feels ~2× stronger than gaining (Kahneman). Upgrade screens: don't list features + "Maybe later" (zero stakes). Show what's at risk — their actual files by name, countdown — and make dismissal own the risk ("I'll risk it"). Status quo bias: people protect what they have.

## 6. Contrast Effect — control what number they see first

Prices are judged relative to the previous number, never absolutely. $50/mo alone = "$600/yr, no thanks". $50 next to a $1,900 laptop = "just 2.6%". Never show a cost in isolation; anchor with a high-value item first (menu Wagyu making the salmon reasonable).

## Applying in review

When reviewing a flow, check: blank fields? progress starts at 0? signup before value? nothing user-owned pre-signup? upgrade framed as gain-only? price shown without anchor? Each hit = concrete fix from the matching principle above.
